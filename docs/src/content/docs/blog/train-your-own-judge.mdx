[Agent-EvalKit](https://github.com/awslabs/Agent-EvalKit) helps you plan an evaluation, collect traces, and score them with LLM-as-a-judge. Most teams use a
frontier model as that judge. A small model fine-tuned for judging can come close to a frontier judge at a
fraction of the cost, and you can host it next to your agents. The hard part is the training data: you need many
rubrics, many traces, and a trusted score for every pair.

This post walks through the pipelines we built to make that data, and the recipe we used to train on it. We are
releasing the code under [`judge/`](https://github.com/awslabs/Agent-EvalKit/tree/main/judge) in this repository, together with [sample records, a sample of the rubric
library, and the list of rubric concepts](https://github.com/awslabs/Agent-EvalKit/tree/main/judge/examples). Everything here runs with any teacher model you can call. In the example
run below, the teacher is [MiMo-V2.6-Pro](https://huggingface.co/XiaomiMiMo/MiMo-V2.6-Pro-MOPD), an open-weight model under
the MIT license, served with [vLLM](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/).

## Why train a small judge

Frontier models such as Claude can already judge agent traces against almost any rubric. The cost is the catch.
Every judgment is billed per token, so teams often score only a sample of their traffic, and the rare failures that
matter most go unread. A small judge you host yourself can afford to read every trace.

Open judge models exist, but each is trained for a narrower job.
[Prometheus 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01535) scores a single response against a custom rubric.
[ToolRM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.11963) scores individual tool calls.
[Web-Shepherd](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15277) scores the steps of web tasks.
[Agent-RRM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22154) scores and critiques whole trajectories so that agents can be trained on
the feedback, and [AgentDoG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18491) flags safety risks. Each learns one kind of target
with one kind of label.

What agent evaluation needs is the job a frontier judge does today: read whatever rubric a team writes, yes-or-no or
graded, and apply it to a whole conversation, a single turn, or a single tool call, in the reply format the
evaluation service expects. We could not find an open model trained for that, so we built the training data for one.

## What a judge has to learn

A judge reads three things: a rubric, the evidence the rubric points at, and an output contract. It returns a score,
usually after a short justification. Some contracts ask for the score alone.

- **The rubric** says what to check and how to score it. Some are yes-or-no. Others are graded on scales of three
  levels or more. Teams write their own, so wording, length, and quality vary widely.
- **The evidence** depends on the level the rubric applies to. A session-level rubric reads a whole conversation.
  A trace-level rubric reads one turn: the user's request and the agent's answer, with the tool calls in between. A
  tool-call-level rubric reads one call, the tools the agent could have used, and the context that led to it.
- **The output contract** fixes the reply format: a JSON object, key-value lines, XML tags, or a bare score, depending
  on what the evaluation service parses.

So the training data needs breadth on all three axes. That is what the pipelines are for.

<Aside title="A note on terms">
We use **trace** the way Agent-EvalKit always has: the record of one request and everything the agent did to answer
it, including its tool calls. Research papers usually call this a **trajectory**. A **session** is a conversation
made of several traces, and a **tool call** is one step inside a trace. These are the units
[Amazon Bedrock AgentCore Evaluations](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock-agentcore/latest/devguide/evaluations-terminology.html) scores at, and they match what tracing tools
already use: [OpenTelemetry](https://github.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai) records agent work as
traces with `invoke_agent` and `execute_tool` spans and identifies a conversation with `gen_ai.conversation.id`, and
[Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/docs/observability/data-model) groups traces into sessions. In rubric files the three
levels are the values `SESSION`, `TRACE`, and `TOOL_CALL`.
</Aside>

## Step 1: Build a rubric library

To judge rubrics it has never seen, a model has to train on many different ones. Ours has 4,597 rubrics over 2,320
concepts, across 16 application domains. It is built in three moves:

1. **Collect seed rubrics.** We gathered about 2,000 from public evaluation datasets and repositories, using Claude
   Code to find and extract them.
2. **Reduce them to concepts.** A concept is one sentence that says what to measure, such as "the agent kept acting
   after the task was already complete". The teacher extracts one from each seed, tags it as session, trace, or tool
   call, and merges duplicates.
3. **Write new rubrics from each concept.** The pipeline samples a scale, label words, a domain, and a writing style,
   and the teacher writes several rubrics per concept in one call, each different from the last.

Why not train on the seeds directly? Public rubrics mostly share one shape: about 90% use a numeric scale, and
only about 6% judge an agent rather than a single response. Rewriting from
concepts lets us choose the shape and the level. The level also tells step 2 what kind of trace to find or write.

Two steps are optional. `gapfill` writes session and tool-call concepts when your seeds lack them, and
`--functional-test` runs a sample of rubrics through the teacher and flags any that give every trace the same score.

```bash title="Step 1 · rubric_build"
# From a concept list, such as the one we release
python -m judge_data.rubric_build --concepts concepts.jsonl --out out/rubric_library.jsonl \
    --presentations 3 $B

# Or from seed rubrics of your own: extract concepts, merge duplicates, then write rubrics
python -m judge_data.rubric_build --seeds seed_rubrics.jsonl --out out/rubric_library.jsonl \
    --filter-unjudgeable --functional-test 20 $B

# Add session and tool-call concepts the library lacks
python -m judge_data.rubric_build.gapfill --library out/rubric_library.jsonl \
    --out out/gapfill_library.jsonl --presentations 5 $B
```

We release the 2,320 concepts and a sample of 104 rubrics. Point the pipeline at the concept list to rebuild a library
like ours with your own teacher.

## Step 2a: Pair rubrics with traces you already have

If you have a pool of agent traces, `rubric_trace_match` picks traces for each rubric. A rubric about refund limits
should see conversations about refunds, so sampling is stratified to give each rubric a spread of relevant traces. The output is a list of rubric-trace pairs that goes straight to annotation.

```bash title="Step 2a · rubric_trace_match"
python -m judge_data.rubric_trace_match --rubric-library out/rubric_library.jsonl \
    --traces trace_pool.jsonl --cap 6 --out out/match $B
```

The trace pool is any JSONL file in the pipeline's trace schema, which the
[datagen README](https://github.com/awslabs/Agent-EvalKit/blob/main/judge/datagen/README.md) documents.

The catch is coverage. A pool collected for other purposes rarely contains the failures a specific rubric is
looking for, so most pairs end up at the same, unremarkable score. That is the main reason we moved to synthesis.

## Step 2b: Synthesize traces for each rubric

`trace_synth` writes traces for every level of each rubric's scale. Each trace conditions on three things:

- **An agent profile**, which is a real system prompt and tool catalogue. Our run drew on 618 open-source agent
  repositories, so the traces look like what deployed agents do.
- **The rubric's concept**, so the trace exercises the behavior being judged.
- **A target score level** from that rubric's scale, such as "partially grounded" or "looped", given by name and by
  its position on the scale.

With `--verify`, a separate call that is not told the target reads the trace and places it on the scale. With
`--drop-mismatch`, traces that miss their target are dropped.
Because the pipeline picks the level, every rubric gets examples across its whole scale, including the rare failures
that a collected pool almost never contains.

```bash title="Step 2b · trace_synth"
python -m judge_data.trace_synth --rubric-library out/rubric_library.jsonl \
    --agent-registry agent_registry.jsonl --out out/pairs.jsonl \
    --traces-per-level 2 --verify --drop-mismatch $B
```

## Step 3: Annotate

`annotate` takes the pairs from either step 2a or step 2b and assembles the complete judge prompt for each one: the
rubric, the evidence, the tool list, and the output contract. The teacher scores that prompt, and its reply becomes
the training target.

```bash title="Step 3 · annotate"
# Pairs from step 2b: a JSONL file
python -m judge_data.annotate --pairs out/pairs.jsonl --out out/output.jsonl \
    --rubric-library out/rubric_library.jsonl --agent-registry agent_registry.jsonl $B

# Pairs from step 2a: a directory, then split into train and test files
python -m judge_data.annotate --pairs out/match --out out/annotated \
    --rubric-library out/rubric_library.jsonl --strip-pct 30 $B
python -m judge_data.rubric_trace_match.assemble --annotated out/annotated \
    --rubric-library out/rubric_library.jsonl --out out/training
```

For synthesized pairs, annotation also varies how the conversation is serialized and which output contract each
record asks for, so the judge does not tie itself to one layout.

Our example run used MiMo as both the trace writer and the annotator.

## Step 4: Train

[`judge_train`](https://github.com/awslabs/Agent-EvalKit/tree/main/judge/train) is full fine-tuning with [Hugging Face TRL](https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/index) and
[FSDP](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/fsdp.html), with loss on the completion only. One recipe worked
across every size we tried:

<Spec items={[
  ['Epochs', '1'],
  ['Learning rate', '5e-6, cosine'],
  ['Warmup', '5%'],
  ['Global batch', '128'],
  ['Context', '8,192 tokens'],
  ['Thinking', 'off'],
]} />

```bash title="Step 4 · judge_train"
cd judge/train
bash scripts/setup.sh

# Tokenize once on CPU, then train from the Arrow dataset
python -m judge_train.data.prepare --config configs/examples/qwen3.5-9b.yaml --num-proc 32
bash scripts/launch.sh configs/examples/qwen3.5-9b.yaml

# Multi-node: run on every node with its rank
NUM_NODES=4 NODE_RANK=0 MASTER_ADDR=10.0.0.1 bash scripts/launch.sh configs/examples/qwen3.5-9b.yaml
```

The example configs assume 8 GPUs per node and keep the global batch at 128: nodes × 8 × gradient accumulation. If
you run on a different number of nodes or GPUs, change gradient accumulation to keep the product at 128.

On 8-GPU nodes, one epoch over the 492K records took about 5 hours for a 9B model on 4 nodes and about 6 hours
for a 27B model on 16 nodes. The checkpoint serves with vLLM like any other model.

## What we found

We trained [Qwen3.5](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B) at 0.8B, 2B, 4B, and 9B, and
[Qwen3.8](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B) at 27B, all on the MiMo data with the same recipe. We scored
every judge on 58,197 held-out judgments: 25,765 on yes-or-no rubrics and 32,432 on graded ones. For
yes-or-no rubrics we report accuracy. For graded rubrics we report how often the judge picks the exact level, and how
often it lands within one level of it.

<SizeSweep />

**Fine-tuning lifts every size.** Every fine-tuned model scores above its untuned base on yes-or-no accuracy and on
graded agreement within one level. The gains are largest in the middle of the range. At 2B, yes-or-no accuracy rises by 15 points, and at 9B a 95% bootstrap interval
excludes zero for all three gains. At 0.8B the gains are small, which suggests a floor on how much judging a model
that size can learn.

**A fine-tuned 4B judges like a fine-tuned 9B.** The two land within about a point of each other, and both beat the
untuned 9B everywhere. If you need a judge cheap enough to run on every trace, start at 4B.

**Fine-tuning mostly removes far misses.** At 27B the exact-level rate barely moves, but the share of graded
judgments within one level rises from 0.776 to 0.813. The untuned model is not much worse at picking the right
level. It is worse at avoiding answers that are two or more levels off, and those are the errors that mislead.

**The teacher sets the ceiling.** The same pipeline with labels from a stronger proprietary teacher brings the 27B
close to [Claude Sonnet 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude/sonnet) on all three metrics. The 27B trained on MiMo data sits
between the untuned model and that reference. A stronger open teacher should close more of the gap, and the pipelines do not
need to change to use one.

**Short replies come for free.** The fine-tuned judges answer in 150 to 190 output tokens on average. Most training
prompts cap the reasoning at 250 words and some ask for the score alone, and the judges learn that habit. Fewer
output tokens make a self-hosted judge cheaper and faster.

## Try it

1. Read the sample records in [`judge/examples/`](https://github.com/awslabs/Agent-EvalKit/tree/main/judge/examples) to see exactly what the judge trains on.
2. Run the recipe with the sample concepts and agents in `judge/examples/` and a small teacher to check the pipeline
   end to end.
3. Add concepts for your own domain and agents. The rubric library is where your requirements enter the data.
4. Fine-tune with one of the [example configs](https://github.com/awslabs/Agent-EvalKit/tree/main/judge/train/configs/examples). Start at 4B, which judged about as well as 9B in our runs.
5. Serve the checkpoint with vLLM and score your own traces with [`judge/serve/score.py`](https://github.com/awslabs/Agent-EvalKit/blob/main/judge/serve/score.py).

The numbers above come from our held-out benchmark, which is not public yet.
